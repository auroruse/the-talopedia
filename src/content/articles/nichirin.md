**Nichirin** (日輪, *nee-chee-rin*), officially the **Miyamoto Shogunate** (宮本幕府, Aldrige: *Miyamoto Bakufu*, *MEE-yah-moh-toh BAH-koo-foo*), is a country in Northern [[valtheria]]. Bounded by the [[hyperborean-ocean]] to the north and the [[bay-of-serica]] to the east, it borders [[rudania]] to the south and [[guandong]] to the east. With a population exceeding 371 million on its mainland and 167 million in the wider [[Aureate Co-Prosperity Sphere|Aureate Co-Prosperity Sphere]], it is the third-most populous country. Mainland Nichirin is divided into 4 specially administered regions (SARs), 44 prefectures, and 15 autonomous prefectures. The capital of Nichirin and its largest city is [[shinkeisei]], located in the world's largest metropolitan area, which has a population exceeding 38 million.

## Etymology

In [[kaiseido]] cosmology, the sun is the axis of heaven, symbolizing divine authority, moral order, and imperial legitimacy. The name Nichirin (日輪) is typically translated as "solar halo," derived from *nichi* (日, "sun") and *rin* (輪, "ring" or "circle"). It expresses the state's self-conception as a civilizational axis: a spiritual and cultural center destined to illuminate and guide the surrounding world.

Foreign exonyms have varied across languages and eras. The earliest Evrian references to the realm appear not as Nichirin but as *Titanvania* or *Titanvana*, transliterations of the archaic Hezhouan name *Tianwan* (天湾, "Heavenly Gulf"). Rumaic scribes associated the first element with the titans of Rumaic myth, an association without philological basis that nonetheless shaped early Evrian depictions of the realm as a land of giants.

Direct maritime contact during the late [[kuronami-clan|Kuronami Shogunate]] produced forms derived from the endonym itself, transcribed at a period when the initial syllable of 日 was pronounced /ti/ rather than /tɕi/. Rumaic sources from the 1550s onward render the realm as *Nitirina*, from which descend Arvernois *Nitirin*, Reinese *Nitirín*, and Vicilian *Nitirina*. These forms preserve a pronunciation abandoned in Nichirian by the early Miyamoto period. Among Altaic groups in Nichirian territory, phonetic derivatives such as *Nijirin* and *Nizirin* are attested, while early Nabatene trade records render it as *Nashirin*.

The Miyamoto Shogunate (宮本幕府, *Miyamoto Bakufu*) is named after the [[miyamoto-clan|Miyamoto clan]], who established military hegemony under Mukaimasa Miyamoto following the reunification of Nichirin in 1603. The surname Miyamoto (宮本) means "base of the shrine" or "near the shrine," often referring to people who lived near a Kaiseido shrine. *Bakufu* (幕府), literally "curtain government," referred originally to a general's field headquarters, but evolved into a term for Nichirin's military governments. The Miyamoto Shogunate remains the official and diplomatic name of the state, while Nichirin or Tenpū serves as the informal or conventional name for the country.

## History

Metropolitan Nichirin has been continuously inhabited by anatomically modern humans since approximately 45,000 years ago, during the Upper Paleolithic period. Archaeological sites reveal a succession of early hunter-gatherer societies that developed into semi-sedentary cultures in the early Holocene. By the 3rd millennium BCE, Neolithic settlements had emerged, practicing rudimentary agriculture and long-distance trade. Bronze and iron metallurgy appeared by the 1st millennium BCE, likely introduced via overland expeditions from the Mu River valley. The archaeological record for periods before the Common Era remains fragmented due to centuries of seismic activity, seasonal flooding, and tsunamis, which have compromised the preservation of early material culture. While mythological texts claim continuous imperial rule since the 5th century BCE, archaeological evidence suggests that centralized imperial authority emerged much later, with pre-CE artifacts exhibiting patterns consistent with decentralized, chieftain-based societies.

From the early 3rd century CE, [[kusanagi]] of the [[chukyo-confederation|Chūkyō confederation]] began exerting nominal suzerainty over surrounding statelets, marking the beginning of a proto-imperial era. Despite claims of divine descent and universal rule, effective control was limited, with peripheral domains maintaining practical autonomy. Attempts at centralization, such as Emperor Hakuhō's reforms in 645 and the promulgation of the [[yamashiro-code]] in 703, failed due to regional resistance and limited administrative capacity. By the 8th and 9th centuries, real power gradually shifted to the samurai warrior class, while the imperial court became increasingly ceremonial. This culminated in 1185, when the [[hayakawa-clan|Hayakawa clan]] established a military government in [[takarazuka]], formally relegating the emperor to a purely ceremonial status.

The newly established shogunate under Akihisa Hayakawa unified Nichirin and created a distinctive cultural identity, with historical texts and arts flourishing after 1185. A devastating measles epidemic between 1286 and 1289 killed an estimated one-fourth of the population, forcing the shogunate to focus on reconstruction. By the 14th century, the Hayakawa clan's power waned due to internal succession disputes, culminating in the assassination of Shogun Hikozaemon Hayakawa in 1336 by a coalition led by Tadamune Kuronami. The Kuronami regime relocated the military government to [[genkai]] and implemented a decentralized system, but lost effective control by 1551 due to internal factionalism and growing resentment among displaced groups. This led to the [[age-of-chaos]], a period of continuous civil war among provincial daimyo that ended in 1603 when the Miyamoto clan, under Mukaimasa Miyamoto, achieved a decisive victory at the Battle of Shizuhata.

The Miyamoto Shogunate brought prolonged internal stability, greater administrative centralization, and the consolidation of a rigid social hierarchy, with Shinkeisei developing as the political and economic center. The shogunate implemented land reforms, regulated social class distinctions, and promoted cultural life. Throughout the 18th century, the shogunate adopted an interventionist foreign policy, particularly in [[manshu]], leading to costly [[manshuan-campaigns|military campaigns]] between 1764 and 1802 against the Hezhouan Xing Dynasty. These campaigns resulted in a bloody stalemate, exhausted the shogunate's treasury and manpower, and contributed to widespread discontent, grain shortages, and peasant riots in the early 19th century. In 1868, the Imperial Court attempted to reclaim political power, triggering the brief [[bakuraku-war]], which was crushed by veteran shogunate forces. Emperor Kasuga was captured and publicly executed, and symbols of the Imperial Court were dismantled.

The shogunate subsequently became increasingly concerned about internal threats and initiated a program of [[shinseiki-reforms|structural reform]] to eliminate the residual autonomy of regional domains. Between 1870 and 1885, the traditional han system was systematically dismantled, and all provinces were reorganized under centrally appointed governors drawn from the military and administrative elite. Feudal titles were abolished, and hereditary stipends were converted into government bonds. These reforms were concluded by 1894, modernizing the state and laying the groundwork for Nichirin's subsequent industrialization and emergence as a regional power.

## Military

The [[shogunate-armed-forces]] (武幕軍, *Bubakugun*) serve as the military of the Shogun and the ruling Bakufu. It is organized into four principal branches: the Ground Corps (陸軍, *Rikugun*), the Naval Corps (海軍, *Kaigun*), and the Aerial Corps (航空軍, *Kōkūgun*). Supreme command is vested in the High Military Council (軍上会議, *Gunjō Kaigi*), chaired by the Shogun, who holds absolute authority as Commander-in-Chief.

Since its reorganization in the 1894 reforms, the Shogunate Army has participated in numerous conflicts, including the [[great-war|Great War]]. In the years that followed, rising tensions in [[evria]] and Hezhou drove military spending to over $1.9 trillion, placing the Shogunate Army among the most heavily funded in the world. As of 1923, it operates closely alongside the Arlbernian, Ponurvian, Arvernois, and Elvesterian armies, its principal allies in the [[mizuhara-pentapartite]].

## Government

The Miyamoto Shogunate is a unitary hereditary military dictatorship established on June 22, 1603, by Mukaimasa Miyamoto. It is governed by a centralized autocratic regime, the *bakufu* (幕府), in which all political authority is vested in the Shōgun, who serves as head of state, head of government, and commander-in-chief of the armed forces. The office is hereditary within the Miyamoto clan, with succession determined by patrilineal descent and internal dynastic arrangements. Following the administrative reforms of 1894 that abolished the feudal system, the bakufu operated through a ministerial bureaucracy, with executive power delegated to a network of ministries overseen by appointees loyal to the Shogun. These ministries include Interior Affairs, War, Justice, Finance, Industry, and Foreign Affairs. [[list-of-nichirian-ministers|Ministers]] are appointed directly by the Shogun.

The legislative function is carried out by decree, with laws and regulations issued directly by the Shogun and his advisors. There is no representative legislature, and political parties are banned. A state-controlled judiciary operates under the Ministry of Justice, with courts structured hierarchically on local, prefectural, and national levels. While the judiciary handles civil, criminal, and administrative matters, it is not independent in the liberal Evrian sense. Judges are career bureaucrats appointed by the central government, and judicial decisions are subject to review by executive authorities in politically sensitive cases. Before 1868, the Emperor functioned as a symbolic and ceremonial figurehead, performing religious and cultural roles while holding no political power. Following the failure of the imperial court's attempted restoration during the Bakuraku War, the Emperor was executed by order of the Shogunate, and the monarchy was formally abolished.

[[administrative-subdivisions|Administratively]], Nichirin is divided into 4 specially administered regions, 44 standard prefectures, and 15 autonomous prefectures.

Specially administered regions (特別行政区, *tokubetsu gyōsei-ku*) are large metropolitan zones considered vital to national economic performance. Unlike standard prefectures, SARs are directly governed by the national government, with no intermediate-level prefectural administration.

Standard prefectures (県, *ken*) are the standard administrative units of Nichirin. Each is headed by a Prefectural Governor (県知事, *kenchiji*), who is appointed by the Ministry of the Interior Affairs. Prefectures are divided into counties (*gun*), which serve as the lowest tier of administrative organization.

Autonomous prefectures (自治県, *jichiken*) are prefectures that possess recognized ethnic, cultural, or linguistic distinctiveness and have limited authority over cultural affairs, language education, and local religious institutions.

## Economy

The Miyamoto Shogunate is a [developing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developing_country) [state-guided capitalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_capitalism) economy. It is the world's largest economy by purchasing power parity, with a gross domestic product of $15.58 trillion as of 1920. The nation accounted for approximately 10.4% of the global economy in July 1920. The economy is driven by close cooperation between the government and private enterprises, with the state playing an active role in industrial development through targeted investment and policies. Large family-controlled industrial conglomerates, known as [[list-of-notable-nichirian-companies|zaibatsu]] (財閥), dominate key sectors and work closely with government ministries to coordinate national economic development. The Nichirian economy exhibits a dual structure, with modern industrial sectors concentrated in urban areas (e.g., Shinkeisei, Takarazuka, [[naginomiya]], [[mizuhara]]) while traditional agricultural and craft sectors are concentrated in rural regions.

The industrial sector has experienced remarkable growth in recent decades, driven by government-sponsored modernization programs and the adoption of foreign technologies. The manufacturing industry is the backbone of the economy, with sub-industries such as textiles, steel, shipbuilding, aviation, and chemicals accounting for the majority of Nichirin's economic output. Heavy industries are concentrated in urban areas, with each of Nichirin's four Special Administrative Regions (SARs) serving as a specialized industrial cluster. For example, the Mizuhara SAR and the mineral-rich wider downstream [[shizuku-river|Shizuku]] region is a center of steel production and ore milling, the Naginomiya SAR and the wider peninsular [[tohanto]] region is a center of shipbuilding, the Takarazuka SAR is a center of chemical, automotive, and aircraft production, and the Shinkeisei SAR is a center of textile production, though most of its economic output comes from the service industry.

Extensive bases of natural resources in Nichirin further fuel its economic growth. Manshu, the nation's industrial heartland, extracts vast quantities of iron ore, coal, manganese, molybdenum, zinc, lead, gold, and graphite to supply its thriving heavy industries. The region also contains large swathes of fertile black earth (chernozem), yielding substantial harvests of wheat, soybeans, corn, and other grains for domestic use and export. The [Daikei Oil Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daqing_Oil_Field) in Manshu, alongside [[karafuto|Karafuto Island]], represents Nichirin's primary petroleum production centers, while the [[chohaku-mountains|Chohaku]] and [[kyoyasu-range|Kyoyasu]] mountain ranges represent Nichirin's primary timber harvesting centers. Tohanto contributes magnesite, coal, and tungsten, while [[sangai]] contributes gold, tin, coal, and timber; however, the natural resources in [[metropolitan-nichirin]] are rather scarce. Hence, Metropolitan Nichirin serves as the nation's financial, commercial, and administrative center, hosting the vast majority of its tertiary sector, while the outlying regions focus primarily on resource extraction and manufacturing, creating a clear economic hierarchy between the core region and the resource-producing periphery.

### Labor Force

Nichirin's domestic labor force in 1920 totaled 198 million people, accounting for approximately 57% of its mainland population. During 1920, 49% of the domestic labor force worked in the primary sector (agriculture, forestry, fishing, traditional crafts, etc.); 34% in the secondary sector (mining, manufacturing, steel production, shipbuilding, textiles, construction industries, etc.); and 17% in the tertiary sector (banking, commerce, transportation, administration, etc.). The unemployment rate in mid-1920 was estimated at 2.8%, down 2.4% from the 5.2% rate in mid-1914, due to strong industrial demand following post-war economic expansion and government-sponsored modernization programs. In 1914, approximately 89.2 million people were employed by 2,847 registered zaibatsu enterprises and their subsidiaries. The eight largest zaibatsu conglomerates each employed between 3.8 million and 6.2 million workers across their operations.

An emerging concern for the Nichirian labor force is the demographic imbalance between rapidly industrializing urban centers and declining rural populations. Rural-to-urban migration has accelerated dramatically since 1900, with approximately 2.3 million workers engaged in seasonal migration patterns between agricultural and industrial employment. While this mobility has supported industrial expansion, it has created labor shortages in traditional agricultural sectors and overcrowding in urban manufacturing centers. While one countermeasure for declining rural populations would be to increase immigration, Nichirin has struggled to integrate foreign workers in agricultural sectors despite labor shortages. Hezhouan, Seignidian, Luconian, Phutopian, and Tonkinese immigrants face significant discrimination from traditionalist rural communities and landowners, who view foreign workers as threats to cultural purity and traditional farming practices. This discrimination has confined most immigrants to industrial work in urban centers, where comparatively progressive zaibatsu enterprises are willing to employ foreign laborers. Government surveys indicate that fewer than 180,000 foreign workers were employed in the primary sector as of 1920, well below the estimated demand of 800,000 additional agricultural workers needed to offset rural population decline. In contrast, the industrial sector has successfully absorbed over 2.1 million immigrant workers in manufacturing and mining.

## Geography

### Topography

Mainland Nichirin is a large nation covering approximately 3,004,368 square kilometers, making it one of the most geographically diverse countries in the world. Its landforms range from alpine ridgelines and floodplains to permafrost highlands and island archipelagos. The country is traditionally divided into four principal regions with distinct characteristics: Metropolitan Nichirin, Tohanto, Manshu, and Sangai. The regions are not official administrative units; each contains numerous prefectures, but they have traditionally been used as regional and cultural divisions within Nichirin.

Metropolitan Nichirin or Nichirin Proper (日輪本国) is a narrow strip of coastal land running along Nichirin's northern seaboard, separated from the interior regions by the [Chohaku Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Changbai_Mountains) (長白山脈) to the east and bounded by the [[haguregumo-river]] (逸雲川) and its source, [[lake-ryusei]] (流星湖) to the south. The terrain is 73% mountainous and is characterized by the [Greater Nichirian Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Alps) (日輪大山脈), a sprawling mountain range that forms the territory's central spine. The range features high elevations, including [[mount-soten]] (蒼天山), the country's tallest peak, and dense temperate forests, which cover approximately 69% of the region. Coastal plains are fragmented and constrained by mountainous terrain, but support high urban concentrations due to their proximity to natural harbors and river mouths. Numerous rivers descend from the interior, forming gorges and feeding into short coastal estuaries. The region lies directly above the Hyperborean-Serican plate boundary, making it one of the most tectonically active parts of the country, with frequent seismic disturbances and periodic volcanic eruptions.

Tohanto (東半島) is a peninsular region east of Metropolitan Nichirin on the northern starboard, separated from Metropolitan Nichirin by the Chohaku Mountains to the west and bounded by the [[karyoku-river]] (苅緑川) to the south. Like Metropolitan Nichirin, the terrain is 70% mountainous, characterized by the [[toketsu-range]] (東傑山脈), a mountain range running vertically through the western portion of the peninsula, interspersed with river valleys and arable plains on both coasts. Flanking the range are two distinct coastal regions: the eastern lowlands, shaped by the confluence of the [[nanka-river|Nanka]] (南華川) and [[kyowa-river|Kyowa]] (共和川) river basins, form a relatively flat, densely populated alluvial plain that stretches into coastal deltas and marshes. The western coast, by contrast, is steep and irregular, marked by escarpments and promontories plunging into the Hyperborean Ocean. The peninsula also lies on active fault zones and is subject to moderate seismic activity, particularly in the northern portion, which is located along a minor branch of the Hyperborean-Serican fault system near the Tohanto Isthmus.

Manshu (満州) is primarily composed of the Great Manshuan Plain (満州平原), a vast and undulating lowland that dominates the region's interior. The region is bounded by the Chohaku Mountains and Karyoku River to the north, the Lesser Kyoyasu Range and [[matsuhana-river]] to the west, and the Greater [Kyoyasu Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khingan) to the east. This plain encompasses the Shizuku (雫川), Matsuhana (松花川), and [[yawara-river|Yawara]] (柔川) river basins, forming one of the largest and most fertile alluvial expanses in Valtheria, which is the agricultural core of Nichirin. Elevations across the plain typically range from 50 to 200 meters above sea level, characterized by broad floodplains, loess terraces, and shallow depressions that are prone to seasonal waterlogging and marsh formation. The plain itself is covered by thick loess deposits, particularly along the eastern edge, creating highly fertile but erosion-prone soils. Isolated basaltic plateaus and volcanic intrusions sporadically emerge across the region, along with periglacial landforms such as thermokarst basins and patterned ground, which are caused by relic permafrost. Seasonal flooding is common, particularly along the lower Shizuku and Matsuhana river basins.

Sangai (山外) is Nichirin's far southwestern frontier, a sparsely populated expanse of highlands, river basins, and boreal forest. The region is bounded to the west by the Great Spatryan Bight, to the north by the Haguregumo River, and to the east by the Lesser Kyoyasu Range. Western Sangai lies within the subarctic zone and is marked by continuous taiga forest, tundra pockets, and widespread permafrost, especially west of Shinkeisei. Eastern Sangai, by contrast, supports mixed forests and more varied topography due to tectonic folding and past glacial activity. The [Ryōzetsu Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhote-Alin) (嶺絶山脈) run in a broad arc parallel to the Haguregumo River, forming another natural barrier between Sangai and Metropolitan Nichirin. Perpendicular river systems, most notably the [[kokuryu-river|Kokuryu]] (黒龍川) and [[sumikuro-river|Sumikuro]] (墨黒川) Rivers, cut through the highlands, forming expansive intermontane basins filled with wetlands, peat bogs, and seasonal floodplains.

Nichirin also administers several overseas territories and peripheral island territories that are not part of the four principal regions, such as [[horai-territory|Hōrai]], Kinshū, Karafuto, the [[northern-kurile-islands]], and the [[rensei-islands]].

### Climate

Nichirin exhibits a wide range of climatic variation due to its latitudinal span and complex geography. Although much of the nation lies on the same latitude as Spatrya, one of the coldest regions in the world, its climate is markedly milder, especially in coastal Metropolitan Nichirin. This disparity is driven primarily by two maritime influences: a strong warm-water current flowing northward along the Hyperborean Ocean coast, and a semi-permanent offshore low-pressure system centered northwest of Nichirin that disrupts Hyperborean katabatic winds. These factors moderate winter temperatures along the eastern seaboard and reduce the severity of cold outbreaks from the continental interior. Orographic uplift from the Chōhaku and Ryōzetsu Mountains, as well as moisture retention by Lake Ryūsei, also help trap milder air masses in Metropolitan Nichirin. As a result, winters in most coastal areas remain mostly temperate, despite their high latitude, although frost and snowfall are still common inland and at higher elevations.

Regional variation exists, though. The westernmost parts of Metropolitan Nichirin, including Shinkeisei, still experience prolonged freezing temperatures, heavy snow, and subarctic influences. Tohantō sees a mix of humid continental and maritime climates, while Manshū is more continental, with greater thermal extremes. Sangai features subarctic to boreal conditions, particularly in its interior, while its northern and eastern river valleys are more similar to those of Manshū, softened by maritime airflow. The Rensei Islands remain temperate year-round. In contrast, Karafuto and the northern Kurile Islands endure long, snowy winters and cool summers, shaped by cold currents and polar winds from the north.

According to the Köppen climate classification, coastal Nichirin Proper, coastal Tohantō, and the Rensei Islands have a Cfa (humid subtropical) climate; inland Tohantō, most of Manshū, and the river valleys of Sangai have a Dwa/Dwb (humid continental with dry winters) climate; inland Nichirin Proper, the Chōhaku Mountains, interior Sangai, Karafuto, and the Northern Kurile Islands have a Dfb/Dfc climate characterized by cool summers and cold, snowy winters; high elevations in the Greater Nichirian Alps and parts of Sangai have an ET (tundra) climate with short, cool summers and long, harsh winters.

### Human Geography

Nichirin's population distribution closely follows its topography. The majority of people reside along the coastal plains of Metropolitan Nichirin and the fertile river valleys of Manshū and Tohantō, where dense urban centers have developed due to favorable terrain and climate. The Satsuno-Sekiringaku Line, running from Satsuno, Satsuno Prefecture, to Sekiringaku, Akamine Prefecture, divides Nichirin into two roughly equal sections in terms of geographic area, with areas south of the line being sparsely settled and those north of it being densely populated, in general. The national population density was approximately 160.46 people per square kilometer in 1926, placing it in the mid-range among developed nations. However, this average belies massive regional contrasts. Heavily urbanized metropolises typically record densities exceeding 1,200 people per square kilometer, while interior and peripheral zones generally fall below 50 people per square kilometer. Urbanization continues to rise steadily, caused by rural migration into government-designated cities where industrial employment and public works offer new economic opportunities. Major cities, especially Shinkeisei, Takarazuka, Naginomiya, [[chukyo|Chūkyō]], and Mizuhara, have absorbed the bulk of this migratory influx, expanding rapidly as industrial hubs and administrative centers over the past decade.

## Demographics

### Ethnic Groups

Though it advertises itself as a multiethnic state, Nichirin is demographically dominated by Nichirians, who comprise 88.23% of the national population as of the 1920 census. Among the 11 other recognized ethnic minorities, the [[hezhouans]] form the largest subgroup at 6.82%, followed by the [[orughans]] at 1.11%. The remaining 3.84% consists of smaller indigenous groups such as the [[shoki]], [[manshuans]], [[samagirs]], [[tungus]], [[olchis]], [[orochens]], [[gilyaks]], [[uilta]], and [[qiakala]], most of whom reside in designated autonomous prefectures.

Systematic discrimination exists throughout Nichirin, particularly affecting Hezhouans. The aftermath of the Great War intensified existing prejudices, as Hezhouan communities faced widespread suspicion due to their perceived cultural and linguistic ties to Guandong, which is traditionally considered an "enemy nation". While smaller indigenous groups also face marginalization outside of their autonomous prefectures, the Hezhouans' substantial population makes them the most visible target.

### Languages

[[nichirian-language|Nichirian]] is the sole official language of Nichirin, used nationwide for government administration, education, and the media. The Nichirian writing system uses two types of scripts:

- **Kanji**: Logographic characters borrowed from [[zhengyu-language|Zhengyu]], used for representing words or concepts. Each kanji typically has two primary readings: *Kunyomi*, the native Nichirian pronunciation used mainly for standalone words or native vocabulary, and *Onyomi*, the pronunciation derived from Zhengyu, primarily used in compound words.
- **Kana**: Syllabic native characters split into two sub-syllabaries: *Hiragana*, used for native grammatical elements, inflections, and words without kanji, and *Katakana*, used for loanwords, foreign names, onomatopoeia, and emphasis.

The government officially recognizes eleven minority languages: Zhengyu, [[shokiese-language|Shokiese]], [[orughan-language|Orughan]], [[manshuan-language|Manshuan]], [[samagir-language|Samagir]], [[tungu-language|Tungu]], [[olchi-language|Olchi]], [[orochen-language|Orochen]], [[gilyak-language|Gilyak]], [[uilta-language|Uilta]], and [[qiakala-language|Qiakala]], each of which is an official language in its respective autonomous prefecture. These languages receive varying degrees of institutional support, particularly within autonomous prefectures, where local governance promotes bilingual education. Elvesterian has taken a major role in Nichirin as a business and link language, and is a compulsory subject at the junior and senior high school levels. Nichirian can be romanized into Rumaic script via the Aldrige system, developed by Elysian missionary and linguist Ezra Aldrige in the 1860s.

### Religion

Kaiseido is Nichirin's dominant religion, practiced by over two-thirds of the population as the indigenous faith centered on worship of *kami* (gods), ritual purification, and reverence for ancestral spirits and natural forces. This ancient tradition has been deeply intertwined with Nichirian cultural identity and state ceremonies since the nation's founding, with major shrines receiving government support and maintenance. Shugendōsha, or [Gautamadharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism), a tradition based on teachings attributed to Gautama that arrived from Southeastern [[pelagonia]] in the 6th century, maintains a significant presence and is often practiced alongside Kaiseido in a syncretic manner. A substantial atheist population, particularly concentrated in urban areas, has grown in recent years, reflecting increasing worldwide secularization among younger generations and intellectuals influenced by modern scientific thought. Shenism and [Orkhanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tengrism) survive primarily in their respective autonomous prefectures, despite centuries of attempts at assimilation.

### Citizenship

Nichirian citizenship is conferred *jure soli*, meaning that any person born within the national territory automatically receives citizenship regardless of their parents' nationality or ethnic background. This birthright citizenship was designed to encourage settlement and population growth in sparsely populated frontier regions. However, the law includes several notable exceptions and complications that have generated legal and social tensions. Children born to foreign diplomatic personnel, temporary workers on contracts shorter than two years, and individuals classified as "enemy aliens" during wartime are excluded from automatic citizenship. The Hezhouan community has been particularly affected by these restrictions, as many were retroactively classified as having "questionable loyalties" following the Great War, leading to citizenship reviews and occasional revocations.
