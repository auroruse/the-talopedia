Sehreno (/sɛ.ˈrɛ.noʊ/, seh-REH-noh), formally The Sublime State of Sehreno (Sehrenese: L-Istat Sublimi ta’ Sehreno, /lɪs.ˈtat sub.ˈliː.miː taʔː sɛ.ˈrɛ.noʊ/, liss-TAHT soo-BLEE-mee tah seh-REH-noh), is a country in Eastern [[pelagonia]] and Southeastern [[evria]], formally known as [[nabataea]]. Bounded by the [[erythaean-sea]] around its northern peninsula and positioned on the Western coast of the Eastern [[panthalassan-ocean]], it borders only [[varahmehr]] to the Northeast. Sehreno is divided into 12 Ġemgħat. The capital of Sehreno and its largest city is [[marsakbir]], which has a population exceeding 10 million.

## Etymology

Historically, Sehreno was universally referred to as "Erythaea" across Evria. Likewise, the modern-day ethnonym “Erythaean” was typically used as a demonym for all Sehrenese nationals, regardless of their specific ethnic or tribal origin. This terminology prevailed internationally until 1895. During an international diplomatic summit held in Marsakbir for the festival of Nayrouz (the Sehrenese Nazarist New Year), King Viktoros-Moustafas III formally issued an imperial edict requesting that foreign powers adopt the native endonym Sehreno in all official correspondence and cartography. Following the summit, "Sehreno" and "Sehrenese" were standardized globally as the official terms for the state and its populace.

The exonym Erythaea derives from the Rumaic Erythraeum mare, adopted from the ancient Astriyan Erythrà Thálassa (Ἐρυθρὰ Θάλασσα), literally translating to "Red Sea." As such, Erythaea historically signified the "Land on the Red Sea," and Erythaeans referred to the collective peoples inhabiting the coastal peninsula.

Natively, the endonym Sehreno likely originates from the Gutian triconsonantal root S-Ħ-R, associated with “dawn”, “enchanted/shimmering coast”, and/or “open desert/marches”. In the pre-Crusade vernacular, the region was referred to as Səħrōn (or Săħrēn), meaning “The Dawnland” or “The Lands of the Enchanted Coast”.

When Avernois Crusader chanceries established administrative seats in the 12th century, Arvernois scribes struggled to transcribe Shushtari and Arabi consonants. In diplomatic Rumaic, Səħrōn was rendered as Sehereno or Sehreno. The Avernois suffix -eno (from Rumaic -ēnum / -ēna) was added to the end to denote origin or terrain. This spelling was reinforced by Evrian etymology connecting the name to the Rumaic serenus ("clear," "serene," or "tranquil"), framing the conquered realm in Evrian courtly literature as Terra Serena ("The Serene Land").

In modern usage, while Sehreno remains the sole official name of the state, Erythaea survives in geography and internal demographics. The surrounding body of water and central mountain range are officially designated as the Erythraean Sea (Il-Baħar Eritreus) and the Erythraean Mountains (Il-Muntanji Eritrei), while "Erythaean" (Eritrew) is used specifically for the ethnic groups living on the Erythaean Peninsula (Il-Peniżola Eritreus) to distinguish them from the broader Sehrenese national identity (Is-Sehri).

## History

### Prehistory

Human settlement on the Erythaean peninsula and adjacent coasts dates to the Upper Paleolithic. By the sixth millennium BCE early agricultural villages had appeared along the Erythraean littoral and in the better-watered inland basins, supported by wheat, barley, and small-scale irrigation. During the third and second millennia BCE a network of bronze-age towns emerged, linked by coastal trade and overland routes that carried copper, timber, and later iron. These towns occasionally coalesced into short-lived kingdoms or fell under the influence of larger empires based further north or east, but none of those overlords established lasting direct rule.

### Antiquity

After the Bronze Age collapse the coastal towns and inland basins fragmented. Over the following centuries local iron-age chiefdoms re-coalesced around fortified centers that controlled stretches of the Erythraean shore and the caravan routes inland. By the early first millennium CE one of these polities, the Kingdom of Gutia, centered on the Erythaean peninsula and its highland hinterland around the city of Marsakbir, had expanded into a durable kingdom. It dominated the regional carrying trade, maintained its own script and monumental architecture, and in the fourth century adopted Nazarism as the cult of the court and the cities. For several centuries this kingdom functioned as the recognized power of the Erythraean coast, exacting tribute from smaller neighbors and resisting or absorbing pressure from larger empires to the north. Its later medieval successors would remember it as the foundation of Sehrenese kingship.

### Shahdist Conquests

In the seventh century Shahadist armies advancing along the Erythraean and Nabataean corridors overran the lowland and coastal territories of the Kingdom of Gutia. The fortified centers that had anchored Gutian power fell in succession; Marsakbir itself was taken after a brief siege. Shahadist governors were installed in the major cities, and Arabi rapidly became the language of administration, taxation, and long-distance commerce. Large segments of the urban and lowland population converted or accommodated to the new order, while Nazarist communities persisted under protected but subordinate status, particularly in the highland hinterland. Over the following two centuries the old Gutian kingdom ceased to exist as a political entity. Its former elites either entered Shahadist service or retreated into reduced ecclesiastical and landed roles.

### Taifa Era

By the ninth century the political unity established by the initial Shahadist conquest had broken down. Authority devolved onto a shifting constellation of regional dynasties and military households, later termed the taifas. Each taifa typically controlled one major port or agricultural basin together with its immediate hinterland, financing itself through customs duties, land taxes, and tribute extracted from weaker neighbors or from the remaining Nazarist highland communities. Alliances were temporary and frequently reversed; a taifa that dominated the carrying trade in one decade could be reduced to vassalage in the next. Court culture in the stronger taifas patronized Arabi poetry, jurisprudence, and commercial record-keeping, while the material culture of the cities continued to blend Gutian monumental traditions with newer Shahadist forms. Military power rested on households of slave soldiers and tribal cavalry rather than on any standing army. Succession disputes within individual taifas were common and often invited intervention by rivals. Although some taifa rulers attempted to legitimize themselves through claims of broader Shahadist authority or through marriage ties to more prestigious lines elsewhere, none succeeded in reconstituting a durable overarching state. The era was characterized by chronic low-level warfare, commercial competition among the ports, and the steady Arabization of urban life, while the highland Nazarist enclaves persisted as tributary but unconquered pockets. This fragmented order endured, with only minor shifts in the relative strength of particular taifas, until the opening of the thirteenth century.

### The Crusade

At the opening of the thirteenth century Nazarist powers in Evria proclaimed a crusade against the Shahadist taifas of the Erythraean coast, citing both the earlier fall of the Kingdom of Gutia and ongoing raids on Evrian shipping. In 1204 Crusader fleets and armies seized the principal ports in succession. Marsakbir fell after a short siege; other coastal cities capitulated or were stormed. The taifa rulers were killed, expelled inland, or reduced to temporary tributaries. In their place the Crusaders established a chain of lordships, military-order territories, prince-bishoprics, and free cities. The newcomers, later known as L-Oltremari, formed a small but militarily dominant elite. Surviving Shahadist urban populations were placed under subordinate status or emigrated; rural districts experienced less immediate disruption. Nazarist ecclesiastical structures were restored or newly founded in the conquered cities, and Evrian chancery practices began to overlay the existing Arabi administrative vocabulary.

The first king, Baldwin, of the House of Vlanden, consolidated the Crusader conquests into a single realm after 1204 with its capital at the holy city of [[l-imdina|L-Imdina]]. He secured recognition from the leading Oltremari houses and military orders, arranged his own coronation, and spent most of his reign reducing remaining Shahadist strongholds. Lacking a direct heir, he contracted a marriage alliance with the wealthy widow of the previous ruler of Vicily. The terms of the marriage treaty stipulated that, in the absence of children from the union, the succession would pass to her son Robert, of the House of Altamont, the heir of Vicily. Despite opposition from sections of the Oltremari magnates and clergy, who preferred a candidate already established in the east, Baldwin upheld the agreement. When he died, Robert inherited the throne.

Robert brought with him the administrative habits of Vicily. He promulgated a body of assizes that came to be called the [[assizes-of-l-imdina|Assizes of L-Imdina]] modeled on the Assizes of Geriano: these defined royal justice as superior to baronial courts in a wider range of cases, fixed military and fiscal obligations more precisely, asserted the crown’s right to a share of customs and certain high dues, and restricted the ability of lords to alienate fiefs without royal assent. The code was stronger and more centralizing than the looser customs that had grown up under Baldwin.

The greater Oltremari houses and several military-order commanders rejected the new assizes as an assault on the autonomies they had secured during the conquest. Armed resistance followed. The resulting civil war lasted several years. Royal forces, reinforced by Vicilian knights and foreign mercenaries, held the capital and most of the coast, while rebel lords controlled significant inland lordships and some secondary ports. Neither side achieved a decisive battlefield victory. The war ended in a compromised settlement: the assizes remained formally in force and royal castellans stayed in the principal fortresses, yet the High Court retained its veto over new taxation and major alienations, and several leading rebels kept their core estates in exchange for renewed homage.

### The Kingdom of Sehreno

Robert’s successors in the House of Altamont preserved the post-civil-war equilibrium through the middle decades of the thirteenth century. They maintained royal castellans in the principal fortresses, collected the crown’s established share of customs, and upheld the Assizes of L-Imdina as formal law, yet they seldom tested the High Court’s veto on new taxation or large-scale alienations of fiefs. Campaigns remained limited to the containment of inland Shahadist remnants and the protection of coastal shipping. Administrative energy went into the chancery and the ecclesiastical organization centered on L-Imdina, where Evrian notarial practice continued to blend with Arabi usage. The Altamont male line ended in 1268 with the death of the king without a direct heir.

The crown then passed, through marriage and recognition by the High Court, to the House of Luxignan. Luxignan kings ruled from the late thirteenth century until 1458 under the same constraints. They presided over a realm that was already confederated in practice: the greater Oltremari houses, the military orders, and the free cities exercised direct authority inside their own territories, while the monarchy retained ceremonial precedence, a modest fiscal base, and the right to summon the host for external defense. Succession within the Luxignan line was generally orderly, but the dynasty produced no sustained effort to reverse the limits placed on royal power after Robert’s civil war. When the Luxignan male line failed in 1458 with the death of King John II.

### The Anarchy

The death of the last Luxignan king in 1458 left the succession disputed between his legitimate daughter Charlotte, then fourteen, and his illegitimate son James. Charlotte was initially proclaimed in L-Imdina with the support of a substantial party among the High Court magnates and the higher clergy. James, however, quickly gathered backing from rival lords, elements within the military orders, and bodies of mercenaries. He seized several coastal fortresses and at least one major port, establishing a rival administration that issued its own grants and sought external alliances. Open warfare followed. Control of individual cities and lordships changed hands repeatedly over the ensuing decades; both sides plundered opposing territories and contracted separate foreign debts. The greater Oltremari houses increasingly treated the conflict as an opportunity to consolidate their own local power, minting coin and raising troops with little reference to either claimant.

Charlotte’s military position deteriorated. After a series of defeats she retained only limited territories and was forced to seek refuge and support abroad. In exile she formally ceded her rights to the throne to her cousin the Duke Charles I of the House of Sabaud, a dynasty linked to her by marriage alliance and already possessed of resources and diplomatic standing in Evria. Sabaud agents and, later, small expeditionary forces began to intervene on her behalf, establishing footholds in friendly ports and negotiating with those magnates who preferred a distant legitimate line to James’s continued presence. James maintained his own court and field army for years, but the prolonged fighting drained his resources and alienated several of his earlier backers. By the early sixteenth century his effective authority had contracted to a handful of strongholds.

In 1520 a coalition led by Philip "The Landless" of the House of Sabaud, comprised of legitimist lords, renewed mercenary support, and the remaining adherents of Charlotte’s cession, compelled James’s heir, James III, to terms. The House of Sabaud was recognized as the ruling dynasty. The settlement restored a single monarchy, yet it did so inside a kingdom whose magnates had spent more than sixty years exercising near-independent authority. The new Sabaud kings inherited the Assizes of L-Imdina and the ceremonial apparatus of the crown, but they also inherited a High Court whose practical autonomy had been decisively reinforced by the Anarchy.

### The Kingdom under The House of Sabaud

Across the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the kingdom therefore functioned as a stable but loose confederation. Economic life centered on the ports, where customs duties and transit trade supplied both the crown and the leading cities. Administrative practice at L-Imdina continued the earlier fusion of Evrian chancery forms with Arabi vernacular usage. Succession within the Sabaud line remained intact, and no second interregnum occurred. By the early eighteenth century, however, fiscal pressure from intermittent warfare and the growing cost of maintaining even a modest royal establishment had become visible. By 1769 the monarchy still presided over the Sublime State in name and ceremony, yet real power continued to rest with the High Court magnates.

### The Era of the Princes

In 1769 long-standing tensions between the Sabaud court and the leading magnates broke into open political fragmentation. A dispute over extraordinary taxation and the command of coastal defenses led several powerful Oltremari houses and military-order commanders to refuse royal summons and to withhold customs revenue. The king retained L-Imdina and a handful of fortresses held by loyal castellans, but effective authority outside the capital district collapsed. Regional lords began to govern their territories as virtually independent principalities: they maintained their own armed retinues, negotiated directly with foreign merchants and Evrian agents, and settled disputes among themselves without reference to the High Court or the crown. The period, later remembered as the Era of the Princes, saw the monarchy reduced to a ceremonial and diplomatic symbol whose edicts carried weight only where a local lord chose to enforce them.

Over the following decades the balance among the princes shifted repeatedly. Temporary coalitions formed to resist external pressure or to plunder a weaker neighbor, then dissolved into renewed rivalry. Some lords invested in new fortifications and small standing forces; others concentrated on controlling caravan routes and port traffic. Foreign powers found it increasingly advantageous to deal with individual princes rather than with the court at L-Imdina, offering weapons, subsidies, or commercial privileges in exchange for access. The Sabaud kings of these years issued formal protests and occasionally attempted to mediate, yet they lacked the military or fiscal means to restore central collection of revenue or to compel attendance at the High Court. By the early nineteenth century the Kingdom existed as a patchwork of armed lordships whose only common institutions were the residual monarchy and a shared Nazarist ecclesiastical framework.

### The Century of Humiliation

In 1813 a commercial dispute and a succession quarrel inside one of the coastal lordships furnished the [[elvester|Empire of Elvester]] with a pretext for intervention. An Elvesterian squadron bombarded two ports, landed marines who seized a customs house and a coastal fort, and dictated terms before the fragmented warlord could assemble a common force. The resulting treaty leased a principal port to Elvester for a long term, authorized a permanent foreign detachment, established extraterritorial courts for Elvesterian subjects, stripped the crown and the local lords of tariff autonomy on key goods, and imposed an indemnity. The speed of the collapse and the one-sided character of the peace demonstrated that neither the Sabaud court nor the regional warlords could defend the coast. Contemporary observers and later nationalist writers alike treated 1813 as the moment Sehreno ceased to function as a fully sovereign actor.

Other Evrian powers immediately demanded equivalent privileges. Within two decades additional ports, stretches of customs revenue, and rights of inland navigation had been alienated by individual princes or by the helpless monarchy. Railway concessions followed in the middle of the century: foreign syndicates financed and operated lines that linked the leased ports to mineral districts and agricultural basins controlled by cooperative lords. Mining rights, timber leases, and, later, preliminary petroleum exploration permits were granted on similar terms. Each new instrument was negotiated with whichever local authority could deliver the territory, further eroding any residual claim of the crown to a monopoly on foreign relations. Extraterritoriality expanded with every treaty. Small, professional foreign garrisons, legation guards, railway police, company detachments, etc. became a permanent feature of the concession ports. Occasional naval demonstrations to collect debts or to protect consuls refreshed the pattern of coercion without ever requiring large-scale occupation.

By the later nineteenth century the political geography of the humiliation had stabilized. The Sabaud monarchy in L-Imdina remained the internationally recognized sovereign and continued to receive foreign ministers, yet it possessed almost no independent revenue and could not move troops through a warlord's territory without permission. Real power rested with a shifting set of armed magnates who had transformed their lordships into durable warlord domains. Those who controlled ports, rail junctions, or mineral fields grew rich by selling access; they imported engineers, purchased modern weapons, and built the first substantial industrial enclaves, arsenals, repair yards, textile mills, inside their own territories. The industry was genuine but denationalized, oriented toward foreign contracts and toward rivalry with neighboring warlords. Rural districts outside the concession corridors remained under traditional lordship or tribal authority, lightly taxed and lightly administered. Nationalist and Socialist criticism spread among junior officers educated in the new arsenals, among students in the coastal cities, and among early labor organizers who watched Sehrenese resources and labor serve external interests. Early Gestaltist texts circulated in the same milieu and were read as demands for a higher coordinating authority capable of ending both princely particularism and foreign penetration.

The final decades before the Great War intensified every element of the system. Petroleum discoveries multiplied the value of certain inland concessions and drew fresh waves of foreign capital and advisors. Warlords who held the fields armed themselves more heavily and intervened more openly in the politics of neighboring domains. The monarchy’s occasional attempts at modest fiscal or military reform were blocked either by the High Court remnants or by princes who simply ignored royal edicts. Foreign detachments, though still small, were now stationed at multiple points and answered to governments that would soon be at war with one another.

### The Sehrenese Civil War

The outbreak of the Great War in 1914 converted Sehreno’s fragmented political order into an active multi-sided civil conflict. The foreign powers that held concessions found themselves on opposing sides of the larger struggle; their small professional detachments in the ports and along the railways, deprived of consistent metropolitan direction, began to clash with one another as well as with local forces. Warlord split according to patronage or ambition: some honored existing contracts and received arms from one belligerent, others switched sides or attempted neutrality and were attacked by both. Elements of the regular army, already weakened by decades of princely obstruction, fractured along regional and political lines. Into this arena stepped organized nationalist cells that had operated underground since the later years of the humiliation.

Their most effective organizer was [[nerik-vizari-de-piro|Nerik Vizari De Piro]], a former member of the Socialist Revolutionary movement who had broken with its remaining leadership over questions of national priority. Before the war he had directed armed combat squads responsible for assassinations of concession officials, bomb attacks on railway guard posts, and the intimidation of warlord tax collectors. Before and during the years of clandestine organizing, De Piro produced a substantial body of writing. He published essays, political dialogues, and semi-autobiographical narratives that circulated in samizdat among students, junior officers, and arsenal workers. The texts combined the conspiratorial directness of his Socialist Revolutionary past with a heightened, aestheticized nationalism: celebrations of violence as a clarifying act, attacks on both liberal parliamentarism and purely economic socialism, and invocations of the nation as an organic will that had been mutilated by princes and foreign concessions. Many of his works came to international acclaim, such as The Pale Horse, Memoirs of a Terrorist, What Never Happened, The Child of Pleasure, The Triumph of Death, and Confessions of a Mask.

De Piro expanded the combat squads rapidly in 1914–16, recruiting arsenal workers, deserting soldiers, and urban youths who rejected both Monarchical authority and foreign patronage. The units specialized in sudden strikes against rail junctions, arms depots, and isolated foreign posts, then dispersed before superior forces could react. A successful coastal artillery ambush in 1916 against an Elvesterian-aligned squadron demonstrated that the squads could now coordinate heavier weapons; the action elevated De Piro from underground terrorist to recognized wartime commander. Months later his formations seized a secondary port, expelled its warlord-foreign garrison, and held the city for several weeks under a revolutionary administration. The episode proved that the combat organization could move from assassination and sabotage to territorial control. By 1917 the original squads had been reinforced by mutinous army units and mass volunteers, forming a field force still marked by the aggressive, decentralized tactics of its terrorist origins.

As the civil war reached its climax, the residual monarchical government opened direct negotiations with Elvester. In exchange for an armistice and the withdrawal of remaining Elvesterian detachments from other points, the crown agreed to surrender the city and port of Marsakbir. This outraged many including De Piro, who called the settlement a "Mutiliated Victory".

### The Sehrenese Regency of Marsakbir

De Piro rejected the provisional government’s peace. He assembled the veteran Combat Squads, now named the Shield Society, reinforced by mutinous army detachments and volunteers, and marched directly on Marsakbir. The Elvesterian administration inside the city possessed only a thin garrison. When the columns entered, the combat squads carried out a systematic massacre of the foreign troops and of those local auxiliaries who had remained at their posts. The killing was public, rapid, and complete; no negotiated surrender was offered or accepted. Within days the Sehrenese flag flew over the port and the former concession buildings.

De Piro returned to L-Imdina at the head of the same squads. Crowds and army units treated the recovery of Marsakbir as the true conclusion of the war. The liberal provisional government, already unable to control its own officers, made no attempt to arrest or even censure him. The massacre, far from isolating the movement, rendered De Piro politically untouchable.

### Years of Lead

The liberal provisional government that followed the fall of L-Imdina proved unable to stabilize the country. Demobilized soldiers, arsenal workers, and rural laborers launched a wave of strikes, land occupations, and factory seizures that lasted through 1919 and 1920. Socialist and syndicalist organizers, many of them rivals of De Piro inside the pre-war revolutionary milieu, established workers’ councils in the industrial enclaves and attempted to coordinate national action. In several ports and rail centers the councils briefly controlled hiring, food distribution, and local security. Lockouts by remaining private employers and clashes with warlord remnants still holding inland districts sharpened the conflict. Street violence between socialist militants and nationalist squads became routine. The period, called the Years of Lead due to the massive increase in political violence, demonstrated both the depth of social mobilization the war had released and the provisional government’s incapacity to mediate it.

The Shield Society, expanded by new recruits and tolerated by sympathetic officers, answered the strikes with systematic counter-violence. Combat squads broke up council meetings, burned socialist offices, and assassinated prominent organizers. Pitched battles occurred in the industrial suburbs of the capital and in the recovered port districts. The liberal authorities condemned excesses on both sides yet lacked reliable troops willing to fire on either the workers’ councils or De Piro’s squads. By the end of 1920 the socialist offensive had been blunted, but at the cost of leaving the Fasces as the only armed organization capable of operating nationally. The Two Red Years thus cleared the ground for open revolutionary action: they exhausted the left, discredited the provisional government, and confirmed that power would be decided by the combat squads rather than by elections or negotiation.

### The Falangist Revolution

In the elections of 1920 the [[national-trade-union-falange-of-sehreno]] emerged as the largest party in the legislature, securing a clear plurality though not an absolute majority. Falange deputies, many of them veterans of the Shield Society or syndicate organizers who had rallied to De Piro during the Years of Lead, took their seats amid continuing street violence. The result alarmed the liberal and moderate blocs that still controlled the provisional government’s ministries and the key committees. Fearing that De Piro would convert his parliamentary strength and his intact combat squads into open domination, the cabinet authorized his arrest on charges stemming from the Marsakbir massacre and subsequent squad actions.

The order was never executed. Senior army commanders, many of whose units had fought alongside or defected to the Falange during the civil war and the Years of Lead, refused to carry it out. Several garrison commanders openly declared that they would not move against De Piro. With the army unwilling to act, the liberal ministers lost the only instrument that could have enforced the warrant. De Piro, now untouchable by legal means, called the Shield Society into the capital. The columns occupied the ministries, the radio stations, and the legislature itself. The provisional government dissolved. The revolution of 1920 was accomplished less by a prepared coup than by the army’s refusal to arrest the leader of the largest party, after which the Falange simply took possession of a state whose coercive power had already declined to obey the liberal cabinet.

With the victory of the Falangist Revolution a Totalitarian Developmental Dictatorship was established by De Piro to bring about Falangism.
