## Modern Era (1818-Present)

### Unification and the First Republic (1818-1848)

During the late eighteenth century, revolutionary upheavals swept across Western Evria, toppling the old feudal estates and giving rise to three sister republics across the southern littoral: the **Occitan Directorate** in the west, the **Septimanian Commonwealth** in the south, and the **Maritime Prefecture of Provence** in the east. Though initially allied during the Continental Wars, the three states soon realized that economic fragmentation, internal tariffs, and uncoordinated defense left them vulnerable to larger northern and Iberian powers. On **14 September 1818**, representatives of the three republics signed the **Treaty of Pestelliére**, formally merging into a single sovereign state initially styled the **United Provinces of Carestur**—often referred to by historians as the **First Republic**.

The First Republic operated as a loose, decentralized confederation governed by a weak five-member executive **Directory** and a unicameral **Federal Diet** rotating annually between **Tolosa**, **Barciona**, and **Marsillia**. While this structure preserved the traditional municipal charters, local courts, and provincial liberties of the former sister republics, it proved ill-equipped to manage rapid economic change. Disputes over internal customs duties, incompatible regional railway gauges, and naval funding repeatedly paralyzed the Diet. By 1847–1848, severe agricultural harvests, regional banking panics, and urban worker uprisings in the textile centers of **Languedoc** exposed the impotence of the confederal executive, culminating in the constitutional collapse of February 1848.

### Industrialization, Empire, and the Second Republic (1848-1875)

In the wake of the 1848 upheavals, a constituent assembly drafted a new national charter establishing the **Second Caresturian Republic**, strengthening central state authority while introducing a bicameral parliament and universal manhood suffrage for metropolitan citizens. Freed from internal trade barriers and unified under a single customs and rail grid, Carestur industrialized rapidly around the textile mills of **Languedoc**, the shipyards and merchant houses of **Barciona**, **Marsillia**, and **Bordigal**, and the metallurgy and coal basins of **Septimania**, **Albi**, and **Forez**.

Seeking both strategic control over the western **Mare Internum** and outlets for surplus industrial capital, Caresturian naval expeditions in the late 1840s and 1850s annexed the coastal ports and Tellian highlands of **Numidia** before extending military protectorates toward the Saharan oases. Initially administered by military governors, Numidia gradually transitioned toward limited civilian administration in its coastal departments. At home, however, the Second Republic suffered from sharp ideological polarization between agrarian federalists in the interior;who feared the dominance of coastal industrial capital and Tolosan centralism;and radical urban republicans in the port metropolises. Executive-legislative deadlocks over direct taxation, church-state relations, and colonial expenditures triggered recurring cabinet collapses, ultimately forcing a comprehensive constitutional overhaul in 1875.

### Parliamentary Supremacy and the Third Republic (1875-1934)

The constitutional laws of 1875 inaugurated the **Third Caresturian Republic**, a pure parliamentary regime in which supreme authority rested with the **Third Caresturian Assembly**, reducing the presidency to a largely ceremonial office. Under this system, Carestur completed its trunk rail network, established compulsory secular public education (*escolas laïcas*) that standardized Caresturian literacy, and deepened the economic integration of Numidia;linking the port of **Algernon** directly to mainland credit markets and encouraging large-scale European settlement alongside the indigenous Arab and Berber population.

Yet the Third Republic’s institutional design bred chronic ministerial instability. Without strong executive dissolution powers or cohesive party discipline, coalition cabinets frequently collapsed within months, trapped between conservative rural notables, free-trade maritime liberals, and a growing socialist and syndicalist labor movement.

The outbreak of the **Great War** in 1914 severely tested this fragile parliamentary order. Mobilizing millions of troops from both the Evrian mainland and Numidia, Carestur endured four years of grueling trench warfare along its northern and Alpine frontiers. While the war ended inconclusively in 1918, the immense human toll, postwar currency instability, sovereign debt crises, and waves of industrial strikes paralyzed the Third Caresturian Assembly throughout the 1920s. By the early 1930s, street clashes between militant labor syndicates and uniformed nationalist leagues threatened civil order on the mainland, while parallel tensions mounted between European *colon* hardliners and emerging indigenous nationalist movements across Numidia.

### The 1934 Constitutional Reform and the Fourth Republic (1934-Present)

As regional instability and authoritarian movements spread across Evria in the early 1930s, a cross-partisan alliance of reformist economists, civil engineers, and constitutional jurists organized the **Meridian Renewal Movement** to prevent total state collapse. Backed by broad public exhaustion with parliamentary paralysis, delegates convened the **Constitutional Convention of Tolosa** in the spring of 1934 and voted to abolish the 1875 parliamentary order.

On **1 June 1934**, the state was officially reconstituted as the **Republic of Carestur;** the **Fourth Republic**;establishing a centralized, unitary semi-presidential system designed to balance democratic representation with executive and fiscal stability: The 1934 Constitution created a directly elected **President of the Republic** serving a five-year term with the power to dissolve the lower house, alongside a bicameral Parliament composed of the **577-seat National Assembly** (directly elected for five-year terms) and the **348-seat Council of the Republic** (serving staggered six-year terms across two regional series). To insulate national infrastructure, monetary stability, and long-term fiscal planning from partisan deadlock, the charter established the **Conseil Supérieur de la Compétence**, empowering qualified jurists and technical specialists to review legislative competence and block unconstitutional or fiscally destabilizing measures, working alongside an orthodox **Bank of Carestur**. Moving away from direct colonial administration, the Fourth Republic reorganized Numidia into a self-governing **Autonomous Territory**, culminating in the **Act of Algernon** and the creation of a **180-seat bilingual Territorial Assembly.**

Following the adoption of the 1934 Constitution, **Henri-Laurent de Montcalm** assumed the presidency alongside a minority **FRP–ADT** coalition government led by Prime Minister **Jean-Baptiste Cazals**. Governing from the capital of Tolosa, their administration formally inaugurated the institutions of the Fourth Republic and oversaw the transition from constitutional reform to regular parliamentary rule.
