**Velvetism** is a political ideology whose primary goal is to establish a society governed by rational judgment, technical expertise, and pragmatic decision-making. It advocates preserving individual liberties and local democratic participation while entrusting national political affairs to appointed experts and professional administrators who, it believes, are better equipped to handle the complexities of modern governance. Velvetism is generally associated with technocracy, authoritarianism, political pragmatism, and social liberalism, although its economic and social policies remain flexible depending on the circumstances of their implementation.

Unlike most democratic movements, Velvetism considers popular participation in national politics unnecessary and potentially detrimental to effective governance. It argues that political authority should be determined primarily by competence, not popularity. Velvetist theorists are of the opinion that while citizens should be free to express their opinions and influence matters directly affecting their local communities like neighborhoods and cities, decisions regarding the national economy, foreign policy, defense, and infrastructure should be entrusted to qualified specialists. They argue that political institutions should be judged by their ability to serve the common good, not by their adherence to a particular ideology or their dependence on electoral votes.

Velvetism rejects rigid ideological commitments in favor of rational pragmatism and views economic and social policies as instruments by which governments promote prosperity, stability, and the common good. While velvetists generally favor a mixed market economy and liberal social policies, they do not consider these principles universally applicable. Government intervention, the private sector, social reforms, and economic liberalization are considered legitimate approaches when their practical benefits justify their implementation. Velvetist theory argues that no political doctrine should be followed blindly if there is evidence that alternative approaches would yield better results.

In foreign policy, Velvetism prioritizes geopolitical interests and practical cooperation over ideological loyalty and maintains that governments with different political systems can cooperate if it serves their common interests. Although Velvetism ultimately seeks to replace national, election-based politics with an expert-led government, Velvetist movements can engage within democratic systems to implement reforms, demonstrate their effectiveness, and persuade the public of their principles. Within Velvetism, there are two main factions: Statist Velvetism, which prioritizes national prosperity, sovereignty, and domestic development, and Internationalist Velvetism, which advocates the international spread of Velvetist principles and the eventual establishment of a more integrated world order, potentially leading to a world without national borders.

## Etymology

The term Velvetism is derived from the surname of the ideology's founder, August Velve.

## Theory

**Velvetist Theory** primarily revolves around the idea of *Rational Governance*. While its social and economic ideas may be shared by various liberal, technocratic, and pragmatic political movements, it is the belief that political authority should be entrusted primarily to those most qualified to exercise it, rather than determined by popular opinion, that sets Velvetism apart from these movements. The idea of Rational Governance consists of three principal pillars.

The first pillar is that of **Technocratic Authority**. Velvetist theory states that the administration of a nation requires a level of knowledge, experience, and understanding that the average citizen cannot reasonably be expected to possess across all fields of government. While Velvetists believe that citizens should retain their freedoms of expression and participate in decisions concerning their local communities, they argue that matters affecting the nation as a whole should be entrusted to appointed experts in their respective fields. Velvetists criticize the idea that national leadership should primarily depend on popularity, political campaigning, or the ability to appeal to the emotions of the electorate. Instead, they argue that the most qualified individuals should be responsible for making decisions based on evidence, practical experience, and the long-term interests of society. Velvetist theorists maintain that government should be treated as a profession requiring competence and accountability, rather than a contest in which political power is awarded to those who can gather the greatest number of votes.

The second pillar is that of **Rational Pragmatism**. Velvetist theory argues that no single economic or social doctrine can provide the best solution to every problem a nation may encounter. They reject rigid ideological adherence in favour of policies that can be evaluated according to their practical consequences, effectiveness, and contribution to the public interest. Velvetists generally support a mixed-market economy and liberal social policies, but they do not consider either to be universally applicable in every circumstance. They may advocate state intervention when private interests threaten national prosperity, or favour privatization when competition and private initiative produce better results. Similarly, they may support extensive social reforms when these improve the well-being of society, while opposing reforms they consider ineffective or harmful. Velvetists believe that the purpose of political theory should be to provide a framework for effective governance, rather than to restrict governments to predetermined ideological conclusions. However, they argue that such flexibility must remain guided by rational judgement, competence, and the welfare of the population, rather than becoming an excuse for political opportunism.

The third pillar is that of **Pragmatic Cooperation**. Velvetist theory maintains that international relations should be guided primarily by mutual interests, stability, and the practical benefits of cooperation, rather than by ideological allegiance alone. Velvetists argue that governments with fundamentally different political systems can nevertheless cooperate when doing so advances their respective interests. They regard unnecessary ideological hostility, geopolitical rivalry, and armed conflict as obstacles to economic development and human progress. Although Velvetism seeks to establish a political order in which national governance is entrusted to qualified experts rather than mass electoral politics, Velvetist movements may still participate in democratic systems when doing so provides opportunities to implement their policies, demonstrate their effectiveness, and gain public support. Velvetists consider political institutions to be instruments for achieving effective governance, and as such believe that engagement with existing systems should not be rejected simply because those systems differ from their ideal. While they disagree over the extent to which Velvetism should be promoted abroad, most Velvetists agree that international cooperation should be pursued whenever its benefits outweigh its costs and it does not endanger their fundamental interests.

Most Velvetists share these three principles, although disagreements exist over their implementation and the ultimate ambitions of the ideology. **Statist Velvetists** believe that Velvetism should primarily concern itself with the prosperity, stability, and independence of the nation in which it is established. They argue that every nation must determine its own political future and that attempting to impose Velvetism upon other societies risks undermining the very principles that the ideology seeks to uphold. Statists therefore favour selective international engagement, emphasizing diplomacy, trade, and mutually beneficial agreements while generally opposing unnecessary foreign intervention.

**Internationalist Velvetists**, however, believe that the principles of Velvetism could serve as the foundation for a more peaceful and cooperative international order if adopted by nations across the world. They argue that many of humanity's greatest problems, particularly war, national rivalry, and the inefficient use of resources, arise from political divisions and competing ideological interests. Internationalists therefore advocate the promotion of Velvetism through diplomatic engagement, economic cooperation, and the development of international institutions capable of coordinating policy between nations. Some envision a future federation of Velvetist states, while others aspire to a world in which national borders and sovereign rivalries gradually lose their significance. Although most Internationalists favour peaceful persuasion and voluntary cooperation, disagreements remain over whether the spread of Velvetism should be pursued exclusively through these methods or whether more forceful measures could ever be justified.
